I. INTRODUCTION

I study how AI is entering legal institutions and how to improve the development of legal technology.

I'm motivated by the belief that technology can help us improve access to justice, but critical work is desperately needed to ensure the responsible and safe use of digital systems. I collaborate with the talented researchers at the Stanford Regulation, Evaluation, and Governance Lab (RegLab). My interests include empirical legal studies, AI in courts and legal institutions, legal NLP evaluation, institutional capacity for AI governance, and technical AI safety.

Generative AI in state civil courts

Stanford RegLab, 2026 to present, with Daniel E. Ho. Authority

I lead a team measuring AI-written text in civil litigation across four state trial court systems. We built PhantomDocket, a dataset of filings with verified AI use and matched controls. We found that off-the-shelf detector estimates are unstable across settings, so we developed ScoreMix, a threshold-agnostic prevalence estimator.

Xaliwar, a constructed language

LING 173 final paper.

Xaliwar is an agglutinative language I designed for the multispecies civilization of the planet Xalitʃɔ. It has twenty consonants and six vowels, (C)V(C) syllables, and SOV order. Nouns take honorific and indefinite prefixes and plural, possessive, and case suffixes in a fixed order. An obviative suffix marks the less salient third person: in joʃ ʃɔxi-n adʒanai-tʃa-lin ʒrap-ag-be, “the kid ate the younger sister’s honey,” the sister is obviative. Verbs carry inferential moods for statements the speaker doubts, and a yes/no question is marked only by rising pitch, a pattern younger speakers have started extending to every question, to the annoyance of older ones.

Build a Xaliwar nounroot · plural · possessor · case

Suffixes and glosses follow the paper. The default, galbi-ir-ja-lag, comes from its example sentence “The kid is reading to his/her friends in the room.”

EXHIBIT F

Citation-graph pretraining for legal reasoning

CS 422 research project, with Stanford RegLab.

Does ordering pretraining documents by their citation graph teach a model the structure of legal text? I built a pipeline that extracts cross-references from California statutes, builds the citation graph, and packs documents four ways for masked-language-model pretraining: random, citation-only, hierarchy, and cite-graph. All four performed about the same on a citation-edge ranking probe. The diagnostics explain why: citation edges are sparse next to chapter structure, and most cross-references point to nearby sections that simpler packing already keeps together.

EXHIBIT G

Shuffling subliminal sequences

CS 221M final project, with Evelyn Yee and Amir Zur. Code ↗

A model fine-tuned on numbers written by a cat-loving teacher comes to love cats too (Cloud et al.). A single prompt with “entangled” number tokens can do the same without any training (Zur et al.). We asked what carries the signal: single tokens, n-grams, or the full sequence order. We replicated the fine-tuning effect in Qwen2.5-7B with LoRA, then trained on shuffled copies of the data. Even one swapped pair of numbers erased the effect. In prompting, order mattered less than how many entangled tokens the prompt contained.

Implicit bias in AI-generated discharge summaries

Stanford CS 329R final project. Code ↗

Do AI-generated discharge summaries carry the racial disparities found in clinician-written instructions? Using over 100,000 discharge instructions from MIMIC-IV, I combine fighting-words analysis, word embeddings, and sentiment models, and compare GPT-generated summaries with the originals. The summaries flatten differences in wording while the underlying treatment disparities remain.

MindMap: clustering student responses for discussion

CS 293, Empowering Educators via Language Technology. With Philip Baillargeon and Nathanael Cadicamo.

Teachers need a fast read of what a class is thinking to set up breakout discussions. We compared three ways to cluster short student answers from the ASAP-SAS dataset: zero-shot GPT-4o prompting, embeddings with k-means, and LLooM concept induction. Prompting and embeddings both grouped answers by surface wording. LLooM produced interpretable themes for about six cents per class of 25. No method’s clusters tracked grades, which is what we wanted: groups by idea, not by score. We built a React, Flask, and Supabase app to run the groups in class. The classroom trial is proposed, not yet run.

Syllabus, SLE 99, Man or Machine?

Student-led seminar, Spring 2025. Co-taught with Philip Baillargeon and Andrew Lee.

Technology has been in human history longer than human history itself. As new machines and tools emerge, societies and human interaction change too. This begs the question: What is truly human, and what is machine? In this seminar, we will explore seminal texts in computer science, philosophy, and critical theory seeking to understand our modern life with machines. Case studies will explore Large Language Model consciousness, decision making supported by Artificial Intelligence, and symbiotic relationships between people and technology.
