I. INTRODUCTION

I study how AI is entering legal institutions and how to improve the development of legal technology.

I'm motivated by the belief that technology can help us improve access to justice, but critical work is desperately needed to ensure the responsible and safe use of digital systems. I collaborate with the talented researchers at the Stanford Regulation, Evaluation, and Governance Lab (RegLab). My interests include empirical legal studies, AI in courts and legal institutions, legal NLP evaluation, institutional capacity for AI governance, and technical AI safety.

Detection Objection: generative AI in state civil courts

Stanford RegLab, 2026. Paper ↗ Project page ↗ PhantomDocket ↗

I lead a team measuring AI-assisted drafting in four state trial court systems. Detectors struggle here: a quarter of filing tokens are boilerplate, and one detector flagged under 5% to almost 50% of filings depending on its settings. We built PhantomDocket, 980 filings known to have used generative AI, and ScoreMix, an estimator robust to those settings. In 127,518 complaints, prevalence is near zero before 2023, then rises sharply.

PhantomDocket: court filings with verified AI use

Stanford RegLab, 2026. CC0. Dataset ↗ Paper ↗ Project page ↗

To calibrate a detector you need filings where AI use is known. Judges supply them: when a ruling calls out a hallucinated citation, the filing behind it almost certainly used generative AI. We started from Charlotin’s AI Hallucination Cases Database, found each case’s docket, and pulled the filing the judge flagged. For contrast, we pulled filings by the judge and opposing counsel in the same cases.

Legal Retrieval Benchmark: Bar Exam QA and Housing Statute QA

Stanford RegLab, 2023 to 2024. ACM CS&Law 2025. Paper ↗

Legal research is a retrieval problem that takes reasoning. We released two benchmarks: Bar Exam QA, about 3,000 questions over 900,000 passages, and Housing Statute QA, 6,853 questions over two million statutes from 50+ jurisdictions, which I built from state housing codes. Standard retrievers struggle. A dense retriever that finds 69% of gold passages on general questions finds 7% on bar exam questions. Expanding the query with structured legal reasoning more than doubles that.

  • Natural Questions69%
  • Housing Statute QA24–51%
  • Bar Exam QA7%
  • Bar Exam QA, with reasoning16%

Xaliwar: a constructed language

LING 173 final paper.

Xaliwar is an agglutinative language I designed for the multispecies civilization of the planet Xalitʃɔ. It has twenty consonants and six vowels, (C)V(C) syllables, and SOV order. Nouns take honorific and indefinite prefixes and plural, possessive, and case suffixes in a fixed order. An obviative suffix marks the less salient third person. Verbs carry inferential moods for statements the speaker doubts, and a yes/no question is marked only by rising pitch, a pattern younger speakers have started extending to every question, to the annoyance of the elders. Conveniently, they use the IPA to write their language.

Cite-Graph Pretraining: structure-aware pretraining for legal retrieval

CS 422 research project, with Joshua Rizo.

In-Context Pretraining (Shi et al., 2024) packs related documents into the same training context instead of random ones, and models trained this way reason better across documents. I extracted cross-references from 205 California landlord-tenant statutes, built a citation graph, and packed documents four ways for masked-language-model pretraining: random, citation-only, hierarchy, and cite-graph. Most cross-references pointed to nearby sections that hierarchy packing already keeps together, and the rarer cross-chapter links were too sparse, and too low in our packing priority, to make a difference.

Subliminal Shuffling: what carries a subliminal signal

CS 221M final project, with Evelyn Yee. Code ↗

A model fine-tuned on numbers written by a cat-loving teacher comes to love cats too (Cloud et al.). A single prompt with “entangled” number tokens can do the same without any training (Zur et al.). We asked what carries the signal: single tokens, n-grams, or the full sequence order. We replicated the fine-tuning effect in Qwen2.5-7B with LoRA, then trained on shuffled copies of the data. Even one swapped pair of numbers erased the effect. In prompting, order mattered less than how many entangled tokens the prompt contained. We conclude that subliminal learning is far too sensitive to be a true AI safety concern.

EHR Bias Audit: implicit bias in AI-generated discharge summaries

Stanford CS 329R final project, with Philip Baillargeon. Code ↗

Do AI-generated discharge summaries carry the racial disparities in clinician-written ones? We compared word use by race in over 100,000 MIMIC-IV discharge instructions, then had GPT-3.5 rewrite 8,000 of them. In the originals, White patients are pointed to rehab, therapy, and follow-up care, while Black and Hispanic patients get dialysis and diabetes regimens with less explanation. The GPT summaries flatten the tone but keep the different kinds of care, and drop details like insulin. Identical tone is not equitable treatment; see Wang et al.

MindMap: clustering student responses for discussion

CS 293, Empowering Educators via Language Technology. With Philip Baillargeon and Nathanael Cadicamo.

Teachers need a fast read of what a class is thinking to set up breakout discussions. We compared three ways to cluster short student answers from the ASAP-SAS dataset: zero-shot GPT-4o prompting, embeddings with k-means, and LLooM concept induction. Prompting and embeddings both grouped answers by surface wording. LLooM produced interpretable themes for about six cents per class of 25. No method’s clusters tracked grades, which is what we wanted: groups by idea, not by score. We built a React, Flask, and Supabase app to run the groups in class.

Syllabus, SLE 99, Man or Machine?

Student-led seminar, Spring 2025. Co-taught with Philip Baillargeon and Andrew Lee.

Technology has been in human history longer than human history itself. As new machines and tools emerge, societies and human interaction change too. This begs the question: What is truly human, and what is machine? In this seminar, we will explore seminal texts in computer science, philosophy, and critical theory seeking to understand our modern life with machines. Case studies will explore Large Language Model consciousness, decision making supported by Artificial Intelligence, and symbiotic relationships between people and technology.
